Training vs Inference

Training is school: expensive, done once by the model provider. Inference is the interview: every API call your app makes. Your app only ever does inference, so new knowledge has to arrive through the prompt. You'll prove it, and then practise choosing between prompting, RAG and fine-tuning.

1

Learn

School happens once; interviews happen on every call.

Definition

Training adjusts a model's parameters on large datasets so it learns to predict tokens well; the model provider does it at huge cost. Inference uses the trained, frozen model to generate output for a new input: what happens on every API call.

In plain words

Training is going to school: years of study, very expensive. Inference is answering a question in an interview: quick, cheap per question, using only what was learned at school plus what's written in front of you. Your app only ever does inference.

Three ways to give a model your knowledge

ApproachWhat it changesEffort and costUse it when
1 · PromptingInstructions and examples in each requestMinutes, almost freeAlways first. Tone, format, behaviour (Module 03).
2 · RAG (grounding)Fetches relevant facts at request time and adds them to the promptDays; needs search infrastructureThe model needs your or fresh facts: policies, catalogues, records (Module 06).
3 · Fine-tuningAdjusts the model's weights with your examplesWeeks; data, cost, re-evaluationA consistent style or narrow skill prompting can't reach. Rarely for facts.
Training from scratchBuilds a new modelMonths, serious moneyAlmost never for an application team.
In the real world

Almost every enterprise "chat with your documents" product uses retrieval, not fine-tuning: help-centre bots, HR policy assistants, internal knowledge search. Documents change weekly, retrieval picks up the change instantly, and answers can cite their source. Fine-tuning shows up for narrower jobs: a consistent house writing style, a specialised classification, or making a small model behave like a bigger one on one task.

Common trap

"Let's fine-tune the model on our policy documents so it knows our refund policy." Fine-tuning teaches patterns and style, not reliable facts, and the policy will change next quarter. For facts that change, use RAG: update the document and the next answer is right.

Interview cue

"Training creates the weights; inference uses them. Our apps do inference only, so new knowledge comes in through the prompt, usually via RAG, not by retraining."

2

Do

Prove the model's knowledge is frozen, then practise the prompt/RAG/fine-tune decision.

Drill 01.9

Frozen knowledge vs handed-over facts

~10 minAzure OpenAIPython

Goal: watch a hallucination disappear when you put the facts in the prompt: a tiny preview of RAG, with zero training.

  1. Run 
    """Drill 01.9: knowledge from training vs knowledge handed over in the prompt."""
from llm import get_client, MODEL

client = get_client()


def ask(messages):
    r = client.chat.completions.create(model=MODEL, messages=messages)
    return r.choices[0].message.content.strip()


question = "What is TrainCrest's refund policy if I cancel 10 days before my course starts?"

print("1) WITHOUT the facts:\n", ask([{"role": "user", "content": question}]))

POLICY = """TrainCrest cancellation policy (fictional, effective 1 October 2026)
- Cancel 14 or more days before the start date: 100% refund.
- Cancel 7 to 13 days before the start date: 50% refund.
- Cancel less than 7 days before: no refund, but one free reschedule."""

RULES = "Answer only from the policy provided. If the policy doesn't cover it, say so."

print("\n2) WITH the facts in the prompt:\n", ask([
    {"role": "system", "content": RULES},
    {"role": "user", "content": f"Policy:\n{POLICY}\n\nQuestion: {question}"},
]))

print("\n3) A question the policy doesn't cover:\n", ask([
    {"role": "system", "content": RULES},
    {"role": "user", "content": f"Policy:\n{POLICY}\n\nQuestion: Can I transfer my seat to a colleague?"},
]))
    $ python lab01_09_frozen_knowledge.py
    Expected output (abridged) 
    1) WITHOUT the facts:
 I don't have TrainCrest's specific policy. Many training providers refund in full if you cancel
 7-14 days ahead... please check your enrolment terms.

2) WITH the facts in the prompt:
 Cancelling 10 days before the start date falls in the 7-13 day window, so you'd get a 50% refund.

3) A question the policy doesn't cover:
 The policy provided doesn't mention transferring a seat, so I can't confirm that.
  2. Verify
    • Answer 1 is a guess or a generic deflection: the weights never saw TrainCrest's policy.
    • Answer 2 is specific and correct (50%) with no training at all: only the prompt changed.
    • Answer 3 refuses to invent. Now delete the system message from call 3 and run again: does it start guessing? That one sentence is a guardrail.
Drill 01.10

Prompt, RAG or fine-tune? Five decisions

~10 minDesignAI assistant as reviewer

Goal: practise the decision you'll make on every GenAI project, then use an AI assistant as a sparring partner to challenge your reasoning.

  1. Think

    Decide each case before opening the answers

    1. Traincrest Assist must answer questions about the refund policy, which changes every quarter.
    2. All replies must use TrainCrest's friendly tone and sign off with "Happy learning!".
    3. Learners ask about courses launched last week, after the model's knowledge cutoff.
    4. TrainCrest wants a cheap small model to classify 25,000 tickets a month as accurately as a big model does, and has 20,000 labelled historical tickets.
    5. Trainers want answers grounded in the exact wording of each course's lab guide, with a citation.
    Compare with my answers
    1. RAG. Changing facts: retrieve the current policy at request time.
    2. Prompting. Tone and a sign-off are instructions in the system prompt.
    3. RAG. New facts after the cutoff must come from retrieval (the course catalogue).
    4. Fine-tuning is a reasonable candidate: a narrow skill, lots of labelled data, cost pressure. Try few-shot prompting first and measure.
    5. RAG with citations. Grounding plus source attribution (Module 06).
  2. Ask AI

    Have an AI challenge your weakest answer

    I'm learning when to use prompting, RAG or fine-tuning for LLM apps. For this scenario I chose
<your choice> because <your reason>:
"<paste one scenario>"
Play a sceptical senior engineer. Give the strongest argument against my choice, one risk I
haven't considered, and what I should measure to decide. Under 150 words.
    Why this prompt works: it gives a role with a job (challenge, not agree), your actual reasoning to critique, and a tight output shape. Asking a model to argue against you is a great way to avoid confirmation bias.
3

Check

Quick decisions.

1. A colleague wants to fine-tune a model on the course catalogue so it "knows" prices. Good idea?

No. Prices change, and fine-tuning doesn't reliably store facts. Retrieve current prices (search or a tool call) at request time.

2. What is a knowledge cutoff, and how do apps answer questions about events after it?

The date the training data ends. Apps add fresh information to the prompt: retrieved documents, tool results or web search.

Takeaway

Your app can't teach the model; it can only brief it. Prompt first, retrieve facts second, fine-tune last.
