From a wall of text to a form.

Definition

A structured prompt organises content into clearly labelled sections, using markdown headings, XML-style tags or other delimiters, so that instructions, context, examples, input data and output requirements are unambiguous to the model and maintainable by humans.

Unstructured

you are a support bot for rr finance bank and
you should be nice and here is the card policy
lost or stolen cards can be frozen in the app
instantly and a replacement arrives in 5 to 7
business days and express delivery costs $15 but
its free for premier checking and the customer
wrote … reply in under 90 words and never ask
for the pin

Structured

## Role
RR Finance Assist, customer support.

## Policy
<policy>
- Freeze the card in the app instantly.
- Replacement: 5 to 7 business days.
- Express: 2 business days, $15
  (free for Premier Checking).
</policy>

## Rules
- Only the policy above. Never ask for a PIN.
- Under 90 words, warm.

<customer_message>…</customer_message>

Facts worth remembering

  • Markdown headings and XML-style tags both work well with current models; pick one convention as a team.
  • Tags around data (<policy>, <customer_message>) are also your first defence against prompt injection (topic 10).
  • For long documents, put the document first and the task last; models tend to follow the most recent instruction best.
  • You can refer to sections by name: "Using only the facts in <policy>…".
In the real world
In finance

Banks use structure to keep three kinds of text apart: the policy (approved wording that compliance owns), the customer's data (their message, their transactions) and the rules (what the assistant may and may not do). Keeping them in separate, tagged sections means compliance can review a change to the policy block without reading the whole prompt, and the customer's text is clearly marked as data. A typical rule set also names the regulated lines: never ask for security details, never promise a refund or a credit decision, and hand fraud and vulnerable customers to a person.

Provider prompting guides recommend this kind of structure, and production system prompts look like small specifications with headed sections and tagged context blocks. Structure also makes prompts diffable: a reviewer can see that only the Rules section changed in a pull request.

Interview cue

"I structure prompts into labelled sections (role, policy, rules, examples, input, output format) and wrap untrusted data in delimiters so it's never confused with instructions."

    On the default llama3.2 (3B) the same run scored 7/10 for the messy prompt and 8/10 for the structured one. Both versions kept telling the Everyday Checking customer that express delivery was free, even after the structured prompt gained an explicit rule ("the $15 fee applies to every account except Premier Checking"). A small model reads "free for Premier Checking" next to the customer's question and over-applies the exception. That's a real finding: structure helps every model, but for rules with exceptions, either use a larger model or move the rule into code (the fee comes from a lookup, the model only phrases it).
    
      
  4. Verify
        
    • The structured prompt passes at least as many checks as the messy one, usually more (often on "hands off non-card topics" and "doesn't waive it for Everyday Checking").
    • The first version of these checks only tested length, secrets, the hand-off and "mentions $15". Both prompts passed them all while giving the customer a wrong, free express delivery. A check that can't catch the failure you care about gives false confidence; write one for every rule that touches money.
    • Read the replies too. Automatic checks catch obvious failures; your judgement catches tone.
    • Run twice. If a check flips between runs, the prompt still leaves room to guess: tighten that rule.

1. Name two benefits of tags around data, beyond readability.
The model can be told text inside the tags is data, not instructions (an injection defence), and you can refer to sections by name in your rules.




Takeaway
Structure your prompts like a form: labelled sections, tagged data, rules you can point to.
