Definition

A clear instruction states the goal, audience, expected scope and length, and success criteria explicitly. Context supplies the background facts and purpose the model needs to make choices the way you would.

In plain words

Say what you want, for whom, how long, in what shape, and why. Then say what to do when the task can't be done.

VagueClearWhat was added
"Summarise this ticket.""Summarise this ticket for the agent who picks it up next: 3 bullets, under 15 words each, include the customer ID and what the customer needs by when."Audience, length, format, must-include items

"Don't be rude.""Be warm and patient. Acknowledge the frustration in the first sentence, then give the next step."What to do, not only what not to do
"Answer the question.""Answer only from the policy below. If it isn't covered, say 'I'll check with the team' and don't guess."Source of truth and an escape hatch

Five habits of clear prompts

  1. State the goal and the audience. "For a customer" and "for an agent" produce very different answers.
  2. Explain why. "Under 60 words because it's shown in a mobile notification" lets the model make smart trade-offs you didn't list.
  3. Be positive and specific. "Use plain English" beats "don't use complicated words".
  4. Separate instructions from data. Put tickets, documents and emails inside clear delimiters (topic 8).
  5. Give an escape hatch. "If the customer ID is missing, use null" stops the model inventing one.
In the real world
In finance

Clear instructions are a compliance requirement in banking, not a style preference. "Explain the fee" is risky; "Explain the $29 overdraft fee using only the fee schedule below, in plain English at a reading age of about 12, without promising a refund, and end with how to request the courtesy refund" is reviewable. Many regulators expect customer communications to be clear, fair and not misleading, so banks write prompts with the same care as a customer letter, and compliance signs off both.

Most prompt "bugs" in production turn out to be missing context: the model didn't know who the reader was, what length fitted the UI, or what to do when information was missing. Teams that write prompts like tickets for a new colleague (background, goal, acceptance criteria) spend far less time firefighting odd answers.

Interview cue

"I make prompts explicit about goal, audience, scope, format and fallback behaviour, and I give the reason behind constraints so the model generalises them correctly."

Vague vs clear: measure the difference

~15 minAzure OpenAINo-code path

Goal: compare consistency, length and usefulness of a vague and a clear prompt on the same support thread.

  1. No-code

    In a chat window, paste the thread from the script with just "Summarise this." Then, in a new chat, use the CLEAR prompt. Regenerate each twice and compare by eye.

  2. Run

        Real output (llama3.2 on Ollama, one run of each, abridged)

    ===== VAGUE =====
--- run 1 · 77 words ---
Here's a summary of the conversation:

A customer, Mei (RR-10293), contacted the airline about a duplicate charge on her cashback card
for a $412 SkyHigh Airlines ticket. The airline's agent confirmed the issue and initiated a dispute...

===== CLEAR =====
--- run 2 · 60 words ---
Here are three bullet points summarizing the support thread:

• Customer RR-10293, Mei, was charged twice for a $412 SkyHigh Airlines ticket on a Cashback Card.
• The agent has raised a dispute for the duplicate payment, but hasn't provided an update.
• Mei needs her replacement card and the refund by 12 Mar, as she plans to travel abroad.
    
        
    The vague run got a basic fact wrong: Mei contacted her bank, not the airline. Nobody told the model whose thread this was. The clear prompt names the reader and the must-include items, and all three clear runs kept the ID, both problems and the deadline (one run called the refund a "rebate", which an agent would correct).

Check
Clarity.



  
1. "Summarise the ticket" gives answers between 20 and 200 words. Which blocks are missing?
Constraints (length, must-include items), output format and context about the audience.

  
2. Why tell the model why a constraint exists?
So it can apply the intent to cases you didn't list. "Short, because it's a mobile notification" also suggests no tables or long links.

Write the brief you'd give a smart new colleague: goal, audience, shape, reason and what to do when stuck.
